\begin{abstract}
\noindent
The HyperTensor volume~\cite{stewart2026hypertensor} showed that a dense
transformer can be compressed and recombined using nothing but the geometry
of its own weights. This paper carries that program to a much harder target:
a trillion-parameter Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) model whose smallest public
distribution does not fit on the hardware it must run on. \emph{DeepSeek-V4-Pro}
is a 1.6-trillion-parameter MoE (49\,B active per token) whose smallest public
GGUF is 697.3\gb{} across seventeen shards, with vendor guidance targeting
multi-GPU servers. We show that the model's \emph{routing} structure is highly
compressible even though its \emph{weight} structure is not: on real code
traffic, $97.66\%$ of expert routing mass on the first dense layer concentrates
in $64$ of $384$ experts, and only $77$ experts ever fire. We use this to build
``keep-$N$'' expert splices --- a $156.1\gb{}$ 64-expert model and a uniform
$16/12/8$-expert ladder --- re-quantize the ladder to IQ2\_XS
($2.36$--$2.38\bpw$, $16.3$--$24.7\gib{}$), and serve them on a $32\gb{}$ laptop
with an $8\gb{}$ GPU at $1.2$--$1.5\tps{}$ generation. Along the way we discover
and fix a metadata defect in the published GGUFs that hangs
multi-token-prediction (MTP)-enabled inference engines forever, and we localise
and fix the two stacked CUDA decode defects that blocked long-sequence GPU
serving: an MMQ scatter-quantise out-of-bounds write (bypassed), and a cuBLAS
fallback invariant broken by repeated expert ids produced by our own
fallback remap (a one-line fix that leaves upstream models untouched). We
also document a third engine defect, \emph{op-offload}, that silently corrupts
CPU-resident quantised tensors on short prompts as well as crashing at
$36{+}$ tokens, and show that \code{--no-op-offload} restores correct
log-probabilities on every domain we test. We
complement the
surgery with \emph{HyperMoE}: a purpose-first domain router, a measured
reconstruction-risk predictor $\rho(h)$ (held-out Spearman $0.98$, tier
agreement $1.0$), and hash-layer prefetch tables, which compose into an
escalation-gated serving dispatcher. Every stage is validated against bit-level
numpy oracles of the full architecture (block forward max relative error
$3.4\times10^{-7}$; full $61$-layer end-to-end serve $5.2\times10^{-6}$). A
task-retention battery measures mean perplexity $2.653$ on the CPU-served
16-expert splice (code $3.000$), and shows the IQ2\_XS GPU tier to be a speed
tier, not a quality tier (mean perplexity $8.540$, degenerate token loops on
all five domains, against $2.653$ at Q3\_K). We
report the negative results as carefully as the positive ones: per-expert
low-rank factorisation, product quantisation, shared-basis factorisation, and
linear router refitting all \emph{lose} on this model, and we explain why. The
thesis is that a trillion-parameter MoE is best treated not as one compression
target but as a \emph{conditional} system --- conditional residency, precision,
and escalation --- with the contract ``compression failure $\Rightarrow$
progressive recovery,'' never silent wrong output.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
% =====================================================================

\subsection{From dense geometry to sparse routing}

The HyperTensor volume~\cite{stewart2026hypertensor} answered one question in
fifteen papers: can you compress and recombine a \emph{dense} transformer using
only the geometry of its own weights, with no training data and no calibration?
Part~I~\cite{stewart2026grc} compressed attention onto the leading eigenspace of
a joint Gram matrix (GRC); Part~X~\cite{stewart2026ceci} spliced a feed-forward
block from one model into another through a shared basis (CECI); Parts~IV and
VIII~\cite{stewart2026ott,stewart2026gtc} cached trajectories on the latent
manifold (GTC); and Part~XV~\cite{stewart2026cog} added a geometric confidence
jury. Those papers worked at the $135\,\textrm{M}$--$7\,\textrm{B}$ scale on
\emph{dense} models.

This paper asks the same question of a fundamentally different object: a
\emph{sparse} Mixture-of-Experts model three orders of magnitude larger, where
the interesting structure is not in any single weight matrix but in \emph{which
experts fire for which inputs}. A dense model reads all of its weights for every
token; a top-$k$ MoE reads a small, input-dependent subset. The compression
question therefore changes shape. It is no longer ``what is the rank of this
matrix?'' but ``what fraction of the expert space does a given kind of traffic
actually touch, and can the untouched remainder be removed, streamed, or
recovered on demand without ever emitting a silently wrong token?''

\subsection{The target and the hardware}

\emph{DeepSeek-V4-Pro}~\cite{deepseekv4pro} is a $1.6\,\textrm{T}$-parameter /
$49\,\textrm{B}$-active MoE in the DeepSeek-V3
lineage~\cite{deepseekv3,dai2024deepseekmoe}: $61$ layers, hidden size $7168$,
$384$ routed experts with top-$6$ selection plus one shared expert, and
$\code{moe\_intermediate\_size}=3072$. Its smallest public GGUF distribution
(BatiAI, Q3\_K\_M) is $697.3\gb{}$ across seventeen shards, measured on disk.
The target machine is a single laptop: $32\gb{}$ of RAM and an RTX~4070 Laptop
GPU with $8\gb{}$ of VRAM --- a configuration the vendor classifies as far below
minimum. The gap between $697\gb{}$ of model and $32\gb{}$ of RAM cannot be
closed by quantisation alone (Section~\ref{sec:negatives}); it is closed by
\emph{surgery plus conditioning}.

\subsection{Research questions}

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{RQ\arabic*.},leftmargin=3.2em]
  \item How much of the $384$-expert space does a given domain actually use,
        per layer, on real traffic?
  \item Can the model be spliced to the used subset without corrupting its
        router, and re-quantised to fit an $8\gb{}$ GPU?
  \item Which classical compression levers (low rank, product quantisation,
        shared bases, router refitting) help, and which fail --- and why?
  \item Can a small, always-resident controller predict a request's
        reconstruction risk well enough to gate escalation to a larger model?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Contributions}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{A routing census of a trillion-parameter MoE.} On $87$ real code
    tokens, the first dense layer concentrates $97.66\%$ of routing mass in its
    top-$64$ experts, drawn from only $77$ distinct experts, $32$ of them never
    in the general top-$64$ (Section~\ref{sec:census}). Hash layers, by
    contrast, spread across $252$--$261$ experts. We give the analogous language
    census (Section~\ref{sec:census}) and mid-layer censuses.
  \item \textbf{Expert splicing (``Degenerate'').} A GGUF surgery tool that
    writes keep-$N$ expert stacks --- a mixed-precision $156.1\gb{}$
    64-expert splice and a uniform $16/12/8$-expert ladder
    ($39.3/32.2/25.0\gib{}$) --- with router-column slicing and identifier
    remapping (Section~\ref{sec:splice}).
  \item \textbf{Two defects in the published artifacts, fixed.} All seventeen
    shards declare one MTP layer but ship zero MTP tensors, hanging
    MTP-enabled engines forever; a one-byte patch fixes it, A/B verified
    (Section~\ref{sec:defects}). We also document a $3$-D importance-matrix
    sizing constraint that aborts requantisation.
  \item \textbf{A quantised, laptop-servable ladder.} IQ2\_XS requants at
    $2.36$--$2.38\bpw$ ($16.3$--$24.7\gib{}$) that load and generate on an
    $8\gb{}$ GPU at $1.2$--$1.5\tps{}$ (Sections~\ref{sec:quant},
    \ref{sec:results}) --- a \emph{speed} tier whose quality we measure
    honestly: at temperature $0$ the requant degenerates on $3$ of $5$ battery
    domains, so it serves only behind a quality gate.
  \item \textbf{GPU decode, unblocked.} We localise the two CUDA defects that
    blocked long-sequence GPU serving and fix the second --- a cuBLAS fallback
    invariant broken by the repeated expert ids our own fallback remap produces,
    a one-line change that leaves upstream models untouched --- re-validating
    $55$-token prefill, $24$-token decode, and log-probabilities on the rebuilt
    fork (Section~\ref{sec:gpudefects}).
  \item \textbf{HyperMoE: an escalation-gated serving stack.} A purpose-first
    router, a reconstruction-risk predictor $\rho(h)$ (held-out Spearman
    $0.98$), and prefetch tables, composed into a dispatcher with a GPU tier
    manager (Section~\ref{sec:hypermoe}).
  \item \textbf{Honest negatives.} Per-expert low-rank factorisation, product
    quantisation, shared-basis factorisation, and linear router refitting all
    lose on this model at any deployable budget; we quantify each
    (Section~\ref{sec:negatives}) and explain the common cause: the expert
    operators are functionally distinct and spectrally flat.
  \item \textbf{Bit-level verification throughout.} A numpy reference of the
    full architecture validates every pipeline stage
    (Section~\ref{sec:oracles}): block forward $3.4\times10^{-7}$, full serve
    $5.2\times10^{-6}$, top-$10$ logits $10/10$, tokenizer $15/15$.
\end{enumerate}

All source, artifacts, and the verification suite are in the UltraTensor
repository~\cite{ultratensor}.

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}
% =====================================================================

\subsection{The DeepSeek-V4-Pro architecture}
\label{sec:arch}

We verified the following facts against the official model definition and the
BatiAI GGUF headers; they are the ground truth for every measurement below.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{Shape.} $1.6\,\textrm{T}$ total / $49\,\textrm{B}$ active,
    $61$ layers, hidden $7168$, $384$ routed experts, top-$6$, one shared
    expert, $\code{moe\_intermediate\_size}=3072$.
  \item \textbf{Two routing regimes.} Layers $0$--$2$ are \emph{hash} routed:
    a token-indexed lookup table (\code{ffn\_gate\_tid2eid}) selects experts
    with zero gating GEMV and zero per-token routing I/O. Layers $3$ and above
    are \emph{dense} routed: a learned gate (\code{ffn\_gate\_inp},
    $7168\times384$, F32) plus a bias (\code{exp\_probs\_b}), a sqrt-softplus
    nonlinearity, a route scale of $2.5$, top-$k$ probability normalisation,
    and weight-before-down application.
  \item \textbf{Expert payload.} Each expert's gate/up projections are stored
    Q3\_K ($9.46\,\textrm{MB}$/expert) and its down projection Q5\_K
    ($15.1\,\textrm{MB}$/expert), so a full $384$-expert stack costs
    $3.38+3.38+5.41\gib{}$ per layer. The routed-expert payload dominates the
    model mass; attention (hybrid compressed/MLA with YaRN~\cite{peng2024yarn})
    is comparatively small.
\end{itemize}

The design consequence is immediate and shapes the whole paper: \textbf{the
model's capacity lives in its experts, and its per-token cost lives in which
experts it reads}. Compression must therefore act on the expert set (which
experts exist) and on the read path (which experts are fetched), not on the
attention geometry that HyperTensor Part~I compressed so profitably in dense
models.

\subsection{What we inherit from HyperTensor}
\label{sec:inherit}

UltraTensor is a direct descendant of the HyperTensor framework, and four of its
mechanisms carry over conceptually even though the object is now sparse:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{Geometry-only compression (Part~I, GRC)}~\cite{stewart2026grc}.
    GRC's discipline --- compress from the weights' own spectrum, then
    \emph{measure} whether it helped --- is exactly the discipline we apply to
    the expert operators. The answer here is largely negative
    (Section~\ref{sec:negatives}), and reporting that honestly is itself a
    HyperTensor inheritance.
  \item \textbf{Component interchange (Part~X, CECI)}~\cite{stewart2026ceci}.
    CECI splices an FFN between models sharing a basis. Expert splicing is the
    intra-model analogue: we cut and re-stack experts of the same model, keeping
    the router consistent with the surviving set.
  \item \textbf{Trajectory caching and manifold navigation (Parts~IV,
    VIII)}~\cite{stewart2026ott,stewart2026gtc}. The lazy, predicted-expert read
    path is the MoE analogue of serving from a cached manifold: fetch only the
    experts the next token needs.
  \item \textbf{The geometric jury (Part~XV)}~\cite{stewart2026cog}. HyperTensor's
    confidence aggregation $J(q,N)=1-\prod_i(1-c_i)$ is the template for our
    escalation gate: a request is served locally when the controller is
    confident and escalated when it is not.
\end{itemize}

The new ingredient, absent from the dense-model volume, is \emph{conditionality
under a hard resource wall}. On the laptop, no static artifact both fits and
retains full capability; the resolution is a system that spends capacity only
when a request demands it.

\label{sec:oracles}

correctly, we first built a bit-exact reference and hold every stage to it.

hyper-compressed mixing, the MLA/YaRN attention, the compressed-attention

\paragraph{Dequantizer and kernel verification.}
UltraTensor decodes every llama.cpp~\cite{llamacpp} GGUF~\cite{gguf} quantisation
format (Q8\_0, Q4\_0/1, Q5\_0/1, Q2\_K--Q6\_K, MXFP4, the full IQ family, TQ1/2,
F16/F32/BF16), each cross-checked against \code{llama-quantize --pure} and the
\code{gguf-py} reference decoders; MXFP4 and IQ2\_XXS reproduce real DeepSeek
tensor blocks at exactly $0.0$ error. The C expert kernel
(\code{ultratensor/kernels/expert\_gemv.c}), a dispatch-aware per-expert
decode-and-GEMV path with AVX2 fused dot products, matches the numpy reference
$9/9$ on real shard experts (\code{tests/test\_expert\_store.py}).

\paragraph{Held-out discipline.}
Every predictor in this paper reports \emph{held-out} performance. The project
learned this the hard way: three separate in-sample headline numbers (an
activation rank of $8$, a controller agreement of $1.0$, a nearest-neighbour
$\rho$) each collapsed on their held-out split at $n=24$ tokens, and only some
recovered at $n=64$--$86$. We therefore adopt disjoint train/projection/
evaluation splits as the standard and do not publish an in-sample number without
its held-out counterpart.

\section{Routing science}
\label{sec:routing}
% =====================================================================

Before any surgery we characterised the router empirically, on real prompt
batteries traced through the real bytes. These measurements decide what surgery
is even possible.

\subsection{Routing structure}
\label{sec:structure}

On layer $3$ over $24$ real tokens (\code{scripts/v4\_route\_stability.py},
\code{outputs/route\_stability\_L3.json}) the top-$6$ selection is almost exactly
uniform: routing entropy is pinned at $1.786$--$1.792$ nats, mean $1.7909
\approx \ln 6$, and the selection margin between the $6$th and $7$th expert
scores is $1.003$ on average ($\le 1.011$ maximum). The keep boundary is, on
essentially every token, a coin flip between nearly equal scores. This single
fact recurs throughout the paper: it is why set-membership is the wrong objective
for a distilled controller (Section~\ref{sec:controller}) and why boundary
disagreements are near-free in output space.

Between consecutive tokens, the \emph{hash} layers churn almost completely:
expert-set overlap is $0.06$--$0.12$ of $6$, i.e.\ churn $0.98$--$0.99$
(\code{scripts/v4\_churn\_analysis.py}). Every hash-routed token therefore
streams ${\sim}200\,\textrm{MB}$ of nearly all-new experts with no page-cache
reuse --- the mechanism behind the measured hash-vs-dense latency gap of
Section~\ref{sec:results}. Crucially, hash routes are a deterministic function of
the \emph{next token}, so a lookahead predictor for hash layers \emph{is} a token
drafter: one mechanism serves both speculative decoding and expert prefetch.

\subsection{Domain censuses}
\label{sec:census}

\paragraph{Code.}
On $87$ real code tokens (\code{scripts/cluster\_code\_census.py},
\code{outputs/code\_census.json}), the first dense layer L3 concentrates
$\mathbf{97.66\%}$ of routing mass in its top-$64$ experts, drawn from only
$\mathbf{77}$ distinct experts, of which $32$ never appear in the general
top-$64$ and $29$ are shared with it. The three hash layers, by contrast, spread
across $261/252/259$ distinct experts with top-$64$ mass shares of only
$0.50/0.53/0.51$ (Table~\ref{tab:codecensus}). This asymmetry is the empirical
license for the keep-$64$ splice: on code traffic, two-thirds of L3's experts
carry essentially no mass and can be removed if the router is kept consistent.

\paragraph{Mid layers.}
A held-out subspace probe over layers L3--L10 (code trace) shows the routed
inputs occupying a growing but still large fraction of the row space: PCA
hold-out at rank $8$ rises from $0.32$ at L3 to $0.71$--$0.74$ at L7--L9
(Appendix~\ref{app:census}). This foreshadows the negative rank result of
Section~\ref{sec:negatives}: there is no small subspace that captures expert
outputs at any deployable rank.

\subsection{Lookahead and speculation}
\label{sec:spec}

With a \emph{perfect} $H{=}4$ lookahead, full hash-layer prefetch coverage is
reached at exactly the $24$-expert union (cap $6\to26\%$ overlap, cap
$12\to52\%$, cap $18\to77\%$, cap $24\to100\%$; \code{scripts/v4\_prefetch\_eval.py}).
A weak bigram drafter plateaus at ${\sim}21\%$ at every cap: for hash layers the
binding constraint is \emph{drafter quality}, not residency.

Speculation cannot, however, be the throughput lever. With the measured verifier
cost $T_V=8.17\,\textrm{s/token}$ (lazy-resident $0.122\tps{}$) and drafter cost
$T_D=1.1\,\textrm{s/token}$, the speculative ceiling with a perfect drafter
($\gamma{=}32$) is $0.754\tps{}$ (\code{scripts/spec\_projection.py}), which
independently matches the batch-$256$ aggregate ceiling of $0.708\tps{}$
(Section~\ref{sec:results}). At a realistic acceptance rate $a\approx0.5$ the
projection falls to $0.12$--$0.15\tps{}$. The verifier's disk-bound read time
is the wall; drafting is the expert-prefetch lever, not the throughput lever.

cause: the expert operators are functionally distinct and spectrally flat, so
there is no shared low-dimensional structure to exploit.}
\label{tab:negatives}
\end{table}

The unifying explanation (Table~\ref{tab:negatives}) is structural. The expert
GEMMs have flat singular spectra and near-zero pairwise correlation in both
weight and activation space; the routed inputs do not live in a small subspace at
any rank a deployment could afford. Every method that assumes shared or
low-dimensional structure --- SVD, PQ, shared bases --- therefore has nothing to
grip. What \emph{does} work is orthogonal to all of them: remove the experts a
domain never uses (Section~\ref{sec:splice}), quantise the survivors uniformly
(Section~\ref{sec:quant}), and predict when to escalate
(Section~\ref{sec:hypermoe}). Capacity (which experts exist) and the read path
(which experts are fetched) are the levers; the internal algebra of a single
expert is not.

% =====================================================================
\section{Expert splicing}
\label{sec:splice}
% =====================================================================

The census gives the license; \emph{Degenerate} (\code{ultratensor/gguf\_keep.py})
performs the surgery. It writes a new GGUF whose expert stacks contain only a
kept subset, in two modes:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{Mixed-$E$ keep-$64$}: per-layer top-$64$ experts by measured
    routing mass, preserving the model's native per-expert precision (Q3\_K
    gate/up, Q5\_K down). Result: $156.1\gb{}$, $4.5\times$ smaller than source.
  \item \textbf{Uniform-$E$ ladder}: a fixed $N\in\{16,12,8\}$ experts per layer
    across all dense layers, chosen so the engine's metadata
    (\code{expert\_count}) is internally consistent. Results: \keepm{16u}
    $39.3\gib{}$, \keepm{12u} $32.2\gib{}$, \keepm{8u} $25.0\gib{}$ --- the last
    $27.9\times$ smaller than the $697.3\gb{}$ source.
\end{itemize}

The non-trivial part of the surgery is keeping the router consistent with the
surviving experts. Degenerate slices the gate weight columns
(\code{ffn\_gate\_inp}) to the kept set, remaps the token-to-expert lookup
tables (\code{tid2eid}) of the hash layers, rewrites the affected KV metadata,
and embeds a provenance manifest (\code{ultratensor.keep\_manifest}) recording
the exact kept-expert lists so any splice is reproducible from its own header.
Because the router's keep boundary is a coin flip (Section~\ref{sec:structure}),
slicing the teacher gate to the kept set is not only simpler than refitting a new
router but strictly better (Table~\ref{tab:negatives}).

\section{Defects in the published artifacts}
\label{sec:defects}
% =====================================================================

Two defects in the public distribution blocked the pipeline; both are now fixed
and the fixes are verified.

\subsection{The MTP hang}
\label{sec:mtp}

All seventeen published Pro Q3\_K\_M shards declare
$\code{deepseek4.nextn\_predict\_layers}=1$ --- promising one
multi-token-prediction (MTP) layer --- yet contain \emph{zero} \code{mtp.*}
tensors. (A positive control, the Flash IQ2\_XXS GGUF, contains $2{,}376$ such
tensors, so our detector is sound; \code{scripts/audit\_mtp.py}.) An engine built
with DeepSeek4 MTP decoding loads the model successfully and then hangs forever
at generation: the MTP loop waits for a draft that can never be produced.

The fix is one byte: zero the flag (\code{scripts/patch\_gguf\_kv.py}). We
verified it A/B on the same model --- with the flag set the model hangs, with the
flag cleared generation proceeds normally. The upstream code paths are
llama.cpp~b10424, \code{src/models/deepseek4.cpp} lines $19$--$27$ and $1409$,
and \code{src/llama-context.cpp} lines $3631$--$3633$. This is a defect in a
widely used public artifact, not in the model weights, and it silently costs
every downstream user their first generation run.

\subsection{Importance-matrix sizing}
\label{sec:imatrix}

Requantising a $3$-D expert stack to an IQ format requires an importance matrix
whose value count equals $n_{e0}\cdot n_{e2}$ (input dimension times expert
count); a mismatch aborts in upstream \code{llama-quant.cpp} lines
$1203$--$1211$. We observed $49{,}152$ vs.\ $1{,}179{,}648$ on
\code{blk.0.ffn\_down\_exps.weight}. The resolution is to synthesise correctly
shaped per-stack importance matrices (\code{scripts/synth\_imatrix.py}); the
single-file splices also required repairing a retained $17$-shard
\code{split.count} claim (\code{scripts/patch\_gguf\_split.py}).

\section{HyperMoE: escalation-gated serving}
\label{sec:hypermoe}
% =====================================================================

The splices are the components; \emph{HyperMoE} is the system that serves them.
It is a routing and escalation layer (\code{ultratensor/hypermoe/},
\code{scripts/hypermoe\_serve.py}) built from three measured parts.

\subsection{Purpose-first domain router}

A registry of eleven specialists (code, math, backend, frontend, data, devops,
and language specialists) is addressed by a purpose-first router that classifies
a request and dispatches it to the best specialist for which a model exists. The
dispatcher supports three backends --- a native \code{llama-cli} path, our numpy
reference, and an HTTP (\code{llama-server}) GPU path --- with a fallback chain
so a request is always served by \emph{some} valid model.

\subsection{The reconstruction-risk predictor \texorpdfstring{$\rho(h)$}{rho(h)}}
\label{sec:rho}

The escalation decision needs a cheap, always-resident estimate of how risky it
is to serve a given hidden state $h$ from a compressed splice. We define the
reconstruction risk as a divergence between the full and compressed next-token
distributions,
\begin{equation}
  \rho(h) \;=\; \KL\!\big(p_{\text{full}}(\cdot\mid h)\,\|\,p_{\text{compressed}}(\cdot\mid h)\big),
  \label{eq:rho}
\end{equation}
and learn a small factored ridge predictor $\hat\rho(h)$ on hidden features. To
avoid the in-sample inflation that repeatedly fooled earlier iterations
(Section~\ref{sec:oracles}), we use a strict three-way split: a projector-fit
set ($128$ tokens), a predictor-fit set ($64$), and a disjoint evaluation set
($64$), with unbiased targets (\code{scripts/v4\_rho\_predictor.py},
\code{outputs/rho\_192.json}). The ridge predictor reaches
\textbf{Spearman $0.9798$, mean absolute error $0.0281$, tier agreement $1.0$}
on the held-out evaluation set; a nearest-neighbour baseline reaches
$0.8785/0.0687/0.9062$. The predictor is rank-robust: a single calibration holds
Spearman $0.910/0.898/0.926$ at ranks $8/16/24$
(\code{outputs/rho\_r\{8,16,24\}.json}), so one $\rho$ serves any rank the
escalation ladder protects.

We report two honest caveats. First, all $64$ held-out tokens in this run fall
in the ``elevated'' risk tier, so the perfect tier agreement is a low-difficulty
result; the $256$-token calibration that would populate multiple tiers is queued.
Second, the two-way (no projector split) variant inflates with rank ($0.85\to
0.44$ Spearman), which is exactly why the three-way design is mandatory. With
those caveats, $\rho(h)$ is operational: it is the gate that decides when a
request must escalate from a splice to the keep-$64$ model or the full path.

\subsection{The distilled controller}
\label{sec:controller}

A companion controller distils the dense router into a small always-resident
predictor. The key methodological finding is that \emph{set membership is the
wrong objective}: because the $6$th/$7$th margin is $1.003$
(Section~\ref{sec:structure}), a controller that regresses expert \emph{scores}
is both better-posed and smaller than one that predicts the top-$k$ set. A
factored ridge on scores holds relative-$L_1$ error $0.086$ (forward) / $0.269$
(reverse) at $649\,\textrm{k}$ parameters --- $4.2\times$ smaller than the router
it replaces --- predicting $91\%/73\%$ of router score magnitude on unseen
tokens (\code{outputs/exp96\_controller\_shrink.json}). Its residual error
concentrates exactly in the near-tied boundaries the router itself cannot
resolve, i.e.\ where disagreement is near-free in output space.

\subsection{Prefetch tables and the GPU tier manager}

For the hash layers, we export the deterministic token-to-expert map as prefetch
tables: $129{,}280$ tokens $\times$ top-$6$ expert rows ($9.3\,\textrm{MB}$,
\code{outputs/hash\_route\_tables.npz}), so the hot experts for a drafted token
are resident before the forward pass. On the serving side, a GPU tier manager
runs exactly one \code{llama-server} at a time on the $8\gb{}$ card and swaps
models on demand; co-resident servers starve VRAM (a co-resident \keepm{12u} fell
to $0.1\tps{}$, solo it runs $1.2\tps{}$).

\subsection{The escalation cascade}
\label{sec:escalation}

The pieces compose into the cascade envisioned by the HyperTensor jury
(Section~\ref{sec:inherit}). An edge splice (\keepm{8u}/\keepm{16u} IQ2\_XS,
$16$--$25\gib{}$) handles the confident majority; $\rho(h)$ scores each request;
risky requests escalate to keep-$64$ or the full path. If the edge handles a
fraction $f$ of requests at rate $r_{\text{edge}}$ and the tail at
$r_{\text{pro}}$, the effective rate is
\begin{equation}
  r_{\text{eff}} \;=\; \Big(\frac{f}{r_{\text{edge}}} + \frac{1-f}{r_{\text{pro}}}\Big)^{-1},
  \label{eq:cascade}
\end{equation}
so shifting the confident majority to the fast edge is the throughput lever the
read path denies to speculation alone (Section~\ref{sec:spec}). The contract is
one-directional: the system may serve a token locally or escalate, but it never
emits a token the risk gate flagged as unrecoverable.

\section{The GPU decode path: two defects fixed, one workaround mandated}
\label{sec:gpudefects}
% =====================================================================

GPU serving originally worked only for short prompts ($1.2$--$1.5\tps{}$);
sequences of ${\sim}45{+}$ tokens failed on the Q3\_K path and long prefills
crashed the IQ2\_XS path. We localised the failures to two stacked defects in
the underlying CUDA engine (llama.cpp~b10424), fixed the second, and
re-validated long-sequence serving on the rebuilt fork. The splice routing
tables are verified valid (indices in $[0,15]$), so the model data was never at
fault.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em]
  \item \textbf{MMQ scatter-quantise out-of-bounds write.} On a locally rebuilt
    engine, \code{compute-sanitizer} identifies
    \code{quantize\_mmq\_q8\_1<D4, scatter=true>} --- the MoE broadcast
    quantisation path ($n_{\text{experts}}>1$, $n_{11}=1$) on sm\_89 --- writing
    ${\sim}144\gb{}$ past a $2\,\textrm{MB}$ allocation ($8.8\gb{}$ before it) at
    ${\sim}45$-token sequences (\code{outputs/sanitizer.log}). Our fork routes
    the MoE broadcast matmuls away from MMQ at both \code{mul\_mat\_id} call
    sites.
  \item \textbf{Broken cuBLAS fallback --- root-caused and fixed
    (20~Aug~2026).} The non-MMQ path for the same broadcast case failed its own
    invariant ($\code{ids\_to\_sorted\_host.size()}=\code{ne\_get\_rows}$,
    \code{ggml-cuda.cu:1990}). Instrumenting the assert and dumping the incoming
    ids matrix showed the cause: the splice's token-to-expert remap routes
    experts dropped from the keep-$N$ set to a single fallback expert ($0$), so
    token rows \emph{legitimately} contain repeated expert ids (a captured row:
    \code{[11, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0]}). The fallback's grouping loop
    \code{break}s after the first slot matching each (expert, token) pair and
    therefore under-produces rows. The fix drops the \code{break}: every slot is
    scanned against every expert, and the per-slot scatter map
    \code{ids\_from\_sorted\_host} --- already sized one entry per slot ---
    carries repeated ids naturally. Models with unique top-$6$ ids (all upstream
    models) are unaffected: the loop produces the identical row ordering.
    Validated on the rebuilt fork: $55$-token prefill, $24$-token decode,
    log-probabilities, repeated requests, server stable
    (\code{outputs/rebuilt\_srv.err.log}).
  \item \textbf{Attention-path crash --- demoted.} An earlier hybrid \code{-ot}
    test (expert tensors pinned to CPU) crashed on the pre-fix engine; the
    Q3\_K \code{-ngl 1} decode hang it was grouped with is now attributed to
    defect~2 (Q3\_K decodes with and without \code{--no-op-offload} on the fixed
    fork). The attention-path failure no longer reproduces and is classified as
    ``needs reproduction'' rather than a confirmed separate defect.
  \item \textbf{op-offload silent corruption (third engine defect,
    20~Aug~2026).} With the engine's default op-offload, ops over CPU-resident
    quantised tensors are scheduled on the GPU. At \code{-ngl 0} this crashes
    with a CUDA illegal-memory-access at $\ge36$ prompt tokens (the original
    reason the CPU battery required \code{--no-op-offload}). We now show it
    also \emph{silently corrupts} on short prompts: at \code{-ngl 12}, three of
    five battery domains collapse into \code{<} token loops with null
    log-probabilities, and prompt processing slows from $0.42$ to
    $0.15\tps{}$. With \code{--no-op-offload} all five domains return valid
    log-probabilities on both the Q3\_K and IQ2\_XS tiers. This is upstream
    scheduling behaviour, not a splice defect.
\end{enumerate}

A related length-dependent crash affected GPU log-probabilities on the original
engine (a CUDA illegal-memory-access in the vocab top-$k$), which is why the
first perplexity battery ran on CPU. Log-probabilities now work on the rebuilt
fork (raw \code{n\_probs} and OpenAI-schema chat log-probabilities both return
entries), which unblocked the GPU battery of Section~\ref{sec:results}; the old
illegal access no longer reproduces, and its exact attribution (MMQ fallout
vs.\ top-$k$) is reported as under test rather than claimed. The net status:
long-sequence GPU decode is verified on IQ2\_XS ($0.6$--$1.5\tps{}$); Q3\_K
decodes on GPU too ($0.07$--$0.15\tps{}$ partial offload); CPU-only serving
remains the stable quality mode, and \code{--no-op-offload} is mandatory on
every Q3\_K tier (CPU or GPU) until the upstream op-offload path is repaired.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% =====================================================================

\subsection{A trillion-parameter MoE is a conditional system}

The organising lesson is that a V4-class MoE is not one compression target but
two, coupled by a router. Its \emph{active-path cost} (${\sim}25\gib{}$ read per
token) and its \emph{stored capacity} ($619\gb{}$ of expert payload) respond to
different levers. Quantisation and residency cut the active path; splicing and
tiering cut the stored capacity; and neither alone fits the model on the laptop.
What fits is a \emph{conditional} system: keep the hot experts resident, splice
away the experts a domain never touches, quantise the survivors uniformly, and
gate escalation on a measured risk predictor, under the contract that a
compression failure triggers progressive recovery rather than a silent error.
This is the MoE-scale realisation of the HyperTensor thesis that geometry plus
honest measurement, not a single clever transform, is what buys deployability.

\subsection{Why the negatives matter}

The failures of Section~\ref{sec:negatives} are not incidental; they are the
reason the surviving design is shaped as it is. Because experts are functionally
distinct and spectrally flat, there is no shared subspace to factor, no codebook
to share, and no low rank to truncate --- so the only free capacity is the
experts a domain does not use, and the only free speed is the requests an edge
model can safely answer. A reader who took only the positive headline would
mis-generalise the method to models where the negatives do not hold; we therefore
give both.

\subsection{The ceiling, stated honestly}

A $1.6\,\textrm{T}$ MoE will not become an always-on few-gigabyte model by
quantisation alone. The asymmetric upside is narrower and real: \emph{fit the
function this token needs into tiny VRAM} --- streamed predicted experts, uniform
low-bit survivors, risk-gated escalation, and a recoverable cold path for rare
capability. The laptop does not run the full model; it runs a system that behaves
like the full model on the traffic it was built for and escalates elsewhere.
The cost surface is now measured rather than assumed: the IQ2\_XS requant of the
naive 16-expert splice sits at mean perplexity $8.540$ against $2.653$ at Q3\_K
and produces degenerate token loops on every battery domain
(Section~\ref{sec:results}), so the speed tier earns its place only behind a
quality gate --- which is precisely the escalation cascade of
Section~\ref{sec:escalation}.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
% =====================================================================

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{Fidelity vs.\ task quality.} Oracle errors (Table~\ref{tab:oracles})
    certify byte-correct reads, not task performance. Task retention is measured
    only by the two perplexity batteries of Section~\ref{sec:results} (CPU Q3\_K
    and GPU IQ2\_XS, short completions); broader task benchmarks are future work.
  \item \textbf{$\rho$ calibration scale.} The $\rho(h)$ numbers rest on $64$
    held-out tokens that all fall in one risk tier; the $256$-token calibration
    that would populate multiple tiers is queued.
  \item \textbf{Census breadth.} Censuses are measured on tens to low hundreds of
    real tokens per domain; wider traffic may reveal weak clusters the current
    negatives (expert similarity, shared bases) do not see.
  \item \textbf{keep-$64$ IQ2\_XS.} Blocked on RAM scratch (${\sim}34\gb{}$ for a
    single $384$-expert tensor) on the $32\gb{}$ box; queued on a larger host.
  \item \textbf{GPU serving.} Long-sequence decode is verified on the IQ2\_XS
    tier of the rebuilt fork ($55$-token prefill, $24$-token decode,
    log-probabilities); Q3\_K GPU decode works but is slow at partial offload
    ($0.07$--$0.15\tps$). All Q3\_K tiers require \code{--no-op-offload}: the
    default op-offload silently corrupts short-prompt outputs and crashes at
    $36{+}$ tokens. The MMQ scatter kernel itself remains unfixed upstream
    (bypassed in the fork), and the old attention-path report is demoted to
    \emph{needs reproduction} (Section~\ref{sec:gpudefects}).
  \item \textbf{Single hardware target.} All serving numbers are from one laptop
    GPU; cross-GPU transfer (in the spirit of HyperTensor Part~IX) is future work.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}
% =====================================================================

\paragraph{Mixture-of-Experts.} Sparsely-gated MoE~\cite{shazeer2017moe},
GShard~\cite{lepikhin2021gshard}, and Switch
Transformers~\cite{fedus2022switch} established conditional computation at scale;
DeepSeekMoE~\cite{dai2024deepseekmoe} and DeepSeek-V3~\cite{deepseekv3} are the
lineage of our target, and Mixtral~\cite{jiang2024mixtral} popularised open MoE
serving. We contribute a routing \emph{census} of a trillion-parameter member of
this family on real traffic, and expert \emph{splicing} as a domain-conditioned
capacity cut.

\paragraph{Expert offloading and MoE serving.} Offloading-based
serving~\cite{eliseev2023offloading} and CPU--GPU
orchestration~\cite{kong2024fiddler} stream experts on demand; our lazy C-kernel
read path (Table~\ref{tab:bandwidth}) is a measured instance, and our prefetch
tables quantify the drafter-as-prefetcher design (Section~\ref{sec:spec}) that
such systems assume but rarely measure.

\paragraph{Quantisation.} GPTQ~\cite{frantar2023gptq}, AWQ~\cite{lin2024awq}, and
int8 serving~\cite{dettmers2022int8} compress dense weights with calibration; we
find (Section~\ref{sec:negatives}) that the activation-aware low-rank and
codebook ideas underlying these methods do \emph{not} transfer to V4-Pro's
experts, and that a uniform IQ2\_XS code is the right survivor
(Section~\ref{sec:quant}).

\paragraph{Speculative decoding.} Speculative
sampling~\cite{leviathan2023speculative,chen2023accelerating} accelerates dense
decode; our projection (Section~\ref{sec:spec}) shows that on a disk-bound MoE it
is the expert-prefetch lever, not the throughput lever.

\paragraph{HyperTensor.} This paper is the MoE-scale continuation of the
HyperTensor volume~\cite{stewart2026hypertensor}: it reuses GRC's geometry-only
discipline~\cite{stewart2026grc}, generalises CECI splicing~\cite{stewart2026ceci}
to intra-model expert surgery, borrows the GTC read
model~\cite{stewart2026ott,stewart2026gtc}, and instantiates the geometric
jury~\cite{stewart2026cog} as an escalation gate.

% =====================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
% =====================================================================

We took a $1.6\,\textrm{T}$-parameter MoE whose smallest public distribution is
$697\gb{}$ and served purpose-built slices of it from a $32\gb{}$ laptop with an
$8\gb{}$ GPU, down to a $16.3\gib{}$ coder model at $1.2$--$1.5\tps{}$. The key
enabler is empirical: the model's routing concentrates sharply by domain
($97.66\%$ of code mass in $64$ of $384$ experts) even though its weights resist
every classical compression lever. We turned that asymmetry into a pipeline ---
census, splice, quantise, route, and gate --- validated end-to-end against
bit-level oracles, fixed a hang-inducing metadata defect in the published
artifacts and a CUDA decode defect in the serving engine along the way,
and reported the negative results as carefully as the positive ones. The broader
claim, inherited from HyperTensor and sharpened at MoE scale, is that a
trillion-parameter model is best served not as a single compressed blob but as a
conditional system that spends capacity only when a request demands it, and never
substitutes a silent error for an honest escalation.

\section{Defect reports}
\label{app:defects}
% =====================================================================

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em]
  \item \textbf{MTP metadata inconsistency} (all $17$ shards):
    $\code{nextn\_predict\_layers}=1$ with zero \code{mtp.*} tensors (Flash
    control: $2{,}376$). Consequence: MTP-enabled engines load, then hang.
    Fix: flag $\to0$, A/B verified. Tools: \code{audit\_mtp.py},
    \code{patch\_gguf\_kv.py}.
  \item \textbf{Importance-matrix validation} (upstream
    \code{llama-quant.cpp:1203-1211}): $3$-D expert stacks require
    $n_{\text{val}}=n_{e0}\cdot n_{e2}$; observed $49{,}152$ vs.\ $1{,}179{,}648$
    on \code{blk.0.ffn\_down\_exps.weight}.
  \item \textbf{MMQ scatter-quantise OOB write}: \code{compute-sanitizer}
    localises \code{quantize\_mmq\_q8\_1<D4, scatter=true>} writing ${\sim}144\gb{}$
    past a $2\,\textrm{MB}$ allocation at ${\sim}45$-token sequences; workaround
    routes MoE broadcast matmuls to cuBLAS (\code{outputs/sanitizer.log}).
  \item \textbf{cuBLAS fallback invariant} (\code{ggml-cuda.cu:1990}): the
    grouping loop \code{break}s after the first slot per (expert, token) pair
    and under-produces rows when token rows contain repeated expert ids ---
    which the splice's fallback remap produces legitimately (captured row
    \code{[11, 0, 0, 0, 0, 0]}). Fix (fork \code{95fcdad}): drop the
    \code{break}; the per-slot scatter map carries repeated ids. No behaviour
    change for unique top-$6$ ids. Validated: $55$-token prefill,
    $24$-token decode, log-probabilities, repeated requests.
  \item \textbf{op-offload corruption}: with the default op-offload, ops over
    CPU-resident Q3\_K tensors run on the GPU --- crashing at $\ge36$ prompt
    tokens at \code{-ngl 0}, and silently corrupting short-prompt outputs at
    \code{-ngl 12} (three of five battery domains return null
    log-probabilities and \code{<} loops). Fix: \code{--no-op-offload} on every
    Q3\_K tier.
\end{enumerate}
